New to the Talmud?

The bedrock of Jewish thought

The Talmud is a vast, 1,500-year-old record of rabbis debating how to live a Jewish life — the source nearly every prayer, holiday, and ritual traces back to. Each page has two layers: a short statement of law called the Mishnah, wrapped in the Gemara, generations of discussion teasing out what it means. You don’t need Hebrew, Aramaic, or any background — every page comes explained in plain English.

From inside the text

Meet the sages

The rabbis you’ll encounter on every page — tap one for a quick primer on who they are and why they matter.

Rashi

1040–1105 · France

Daf: Berakhot 2a

The most influential Talmud commentator. His commentary appears on every page.

Period: Rishon · 11th–12th century

Based in: Troyes, France

Known for: Line-by-line commentary that made the Talmud accessible to all — printed on every page ever since

Debates with: The Tosafists — his own grandsons and students, who challenge him in the outer column

A teaching: Rashi never assumed his reader already knew. He explained everything, from the simplest word to the most tangled legal argument. That radical clarity is his legacy.

On the page: The narrow inner column on every page is always Rashi. The distinctive slanted typeface — “Rashi script” — was named for him. Once you recognize it, you’ll never miss him.

Rabbi Akiva

c. 50–135 CE

Daf: Berakhot 61b

Shepherd turned sage. Foundational figure of rabbinic Judaism.

Period: Tanna · 3rd generation

Based in: Bnei Brak, Israel

Known for: Deriving new laws from every letter and ornament of the Torah; martyrdom under Rome

Debates with: Rabbi Ishmael, who argued Akiva read too much into the text

A teaching: “Love your neighbor as yourself — this is the great principle of the entire Torah.” (Yerushalmi Nedarim 9:4)

On the page: Scholars say the anonymous Mishnah — the passages with no named author — follows Rabbi Akiva’s view. When the Mishnah speaks without attribution, it’s likely his voice.

Hillel

c. 110 BCE–10 CE

Daf: Shabbat 31a

Famous for the Golden Rule. School of Hillel shaped halakhah.

Period: Tanna · Zugot (paired) era

Based in: Jerusalem

Known for: Legal leniency and ethical clarity; founding the School of Hillel that shaped mainstream Jewish law

Debates with: Shammai — the two are paired on nearly every disputed question in the Mishnah

A teaching: “What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbor. That is the whole Torah — the rest is commentary. Go and learn.” (Shabbat 31a)

On the page: “Beit Hillel says… Beit Shammai says…” appears hundreds of times in the Talmud. The law almost always follows Hillel. When it follows Shammai, it’s worth pausing to ask why.

Rava

c. 280–352 · Babylon

Daf: Bava Metzia 59b

Towering Babylonian sage. His debates with Abaye fill the Talmud.

Period: Amora · 4th generation (Babylonian)

Based in: Machoza, Babylon

Known for: Civil and criminal law; his running debate with Abaye is the defining intellectual rivalry of the Talmud

Debates with: Abaye — the law follows Rava in all but six of their disputes. The six exceptions are memorized by a special mnemonic.

A teaching: “A person should always live in the same city as their teacher.” He modeled this himself, remaining in Machoza to study under Rav Yosef. (Berakhot 8a)

On the page: “Rava said” often signals the resolution of a debate. When you see “Abaye said… Rava said…” side by side, expect Rava’s view to win — and to become law.

Rabbi Yochanan

c. 180–279

Daf: Bava Metzia 84a

Compiler of the Jerusalem Talmud, known for piercing legal mind.

Period: Amora · 1st generation (Israeli)

Based in: Tiberias, Israel

Known for: The dominant voice of Israeli scholarship; principal architect of the Jerusalem Talmud

Debates with: Reish Lakish — his brother-in-law and the Talmud’s most beloved intellectual friendship

A teaching: “Whoever recites a teaching in the name of the one who said it brings redemption to the world.” (Megillah 15a)

On the page: When Babylonian and Israeli sages disagree, Yochanan speaks for Israel. His opinions often begin “Rabbi Yochanan said in the name of…” — he was meticulous about attribution.

Beruriah

2nd century

Daf: Berakhot 10a

One of the only women whose teachings appear in the Talmud.

Period: Tanna · 2nd century

Based in: Israel (wife of Rabbi Meir)

Known for: The only woman whose legal rulings are recorded in the Talmud and accepted as authoritative

Debates with: Male sages she corrected on points of law — and her own husband, Rabbi Meir

A teaching: When her two sons died, she asked Rabbi Meir: “If someone entrusts you with a treasure, must you return it when asked?” He said yes. “God gave us these sons and has taken them back.” (Midrash Mishlei 31)

On the page: Her appearances are rare and always deliberate — each one was preserved in a literature that rarely recorded women’s voices. When she appears, it’s worth stopping.

Frequently asked

Do I need to know Hebrew or Aramaic?

Not at all. The Daily Dose is written in clear English. Hebrew and Aramaic terms are explained in context. Many of our most loyal readers have no formal Jewish education or language background.

What if I miss a day or fall behind?

Don’t worry about it. Most Daf Yomi readers miss days. The schedule waits for no one, but the archive is always here, and the next email always lands tomorrow. Consistency over perfection.

How long does each day take?

The Daily Dose is a five-minute read. Studying the actual page in depth typically takes 45–60 minutes, but that’s optional — the email alone is meaningful.

When does the current cycle end?

The 14th cycle began January 5, 2020 and ends around June 7, 2027 with a global Siyyum HaShas celebration. The 15th cycle begins immediately after.
